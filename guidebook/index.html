CHAPTER 01The project in one page

Title: Weather-Aware Multi-Site Short-Term Solar Power Forecasting Across India's MNRE-Sanctioned Solar Parks.

In one sentence: we built a system that predicts how much electricity 43 of India's big government-backed solar parks will produce 1, 3, 6 and 24 hours ahead, tested it honestly on nine months of data it had never seen, checked it on two real plants, and put it on a public website.

43solar parks forecast, at 36 weather locations
968,016park-hours of data, Apr 2024 – Sep 2026
4.5%typical error 1 h ahead (% of park capacity)
15–44%better than the best simple baseline
1.4%error for all parks added together (1 h)
11experiments run

The story in seven steps

  1. Problem. Solar output jumps around with clouds, dust, heat and monsoon. The grid operator must know in advance how much is coming, and Indian rules charge plants for wrong schedules.
  2. Scope. We started with one plant, then widened it to all MNRE-sanctioned solar parks, because single-plant results don't generalise and grid operators care about totals. We verified the list from official records (the famous "42 parks" figure is from March 2019; the latest park-wise list has 50).
  3. Data problem. No park publishes hourly power. So we calculate it from real past weather using a physics library (pvlib). This is our "actual" power.
  4. Honesty rule. The model never sees the real weather. It only sees weather forecasts made 1–2 days earlier, exactly what a grid operator would have.
  5. Models. Simple baselines, then machine learning (Ridge, Random Forest, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost) and deep learning (LSTM, GRU), the heavy ones on a laptop GPU.
  6. Experiments. Eleven experiments answered: which model is best, whether one model for all parks beats separate ones, which inputs matter, whether India's total is easier, how ramps behave, whether new parks can be forecast, whether our ranges are trustworthy, and whether it works on real plants.
  7. Delivery. A FastAPI service, a Streamlit dashboard and a public website show every forecast.
How to say it

"Our system forecasts India's solar parks 1 to 24 hours ahead. Because parks don't share data, we simulate their output with physics, but the model only sees day-old weather forecasts, so it can't cheat. One machine-learning model for all parks beats the best baseline by 15 to 44 percent, works for new parks, and transfers to real plants."

CHAPTER 02Words you must know

Learn these first. Almost every question uses one of them.

MNRE
Ministry of New and Renewable Energy, Government of India. Runs the solar park scheme.
Solar park
A big area where the government prepares land, roads, substation and power lines; many companies then build solar power plants inside it. A park is not a plant. (Example: Bhadla in Rajasthan.)
SPPD
Solar Power Park Developer: the agency that develops the park (often a state company or a joint venture with SECI).
SECI
Solar Energy Corporation of India, a central company that helps implement the scheme.
Capacity (MW)
The maximum power the park can deliver. Sanctioned = approved; commissioned = actually built and running.
Irradiance
Sunlight power per square metre (W/m²). GHI = total on flat ground; DNI = direct beam; DHI = scattered (diffuse) light from the sky.
Clear-sky power
What the park would produce right now if there were no clouds. Calculated from sun position.
Clear-sky index (k)
Actual power ÷ clear-sky power. k = 1 means clear sky, k = 0.3 means heavy cloud.
Capacity factor
Average output ÷ capacity over a year. Our parks: 19.8–24.3%.
NWP
Numerical Weather Prediction: a weather forecast made by a physics computer model. We use ECMWF IFS.
ECMWF
European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. Makes the IFS forecast (0.25° ≈ 25 km grid) and ERA5.
ERA5
A "reanalysis": the best reconstruction of what the weather actually was, every hour, worldwide. We use it as "real past weather".
Open-Meteo
A free website/API that serves ERA5 and archived ECMWF forecasts. We downloaded everything through it.
pvlib
An open-source Python library that calculates solar panel power from weather using physics.
Horizon
How far ahead we predict: 1, 3, 6 or 24 hours (24 h = "day-ahead").
Baseline
A simple forecast that any model must beat. Ours: persistence, smart persistence, climatology, NWP + physics.
Persistence
"Next hour = this hour." Very bad at sunrise/sunset.
Smart persistence
"The sky stays as cloudy as it is now": take today's clear-sky index and apply it to the future clear-sky power. Our main baseline.
nRMSE
Our main error score: root-mean-square error divided by capacity, in percent. Lower is better. 4.5% on a 2,000 MW park ≈ 90 MW typical error.
Skill
How much better than smart persistence: 1 − (our error ÷ baseline error). 34% skill = 34% less error.
Global model
One model trained on all parks together (the opposite of one model per park).
Feature
One input column the model uses, e.g. "power 1 hour ago" or "forecast cloud cover".
GPU / CUDA
Graphics card and NVIDIA's software for running maths on it. We trained XGBoost, CatBoost, LSTM, GRU on an RTX 4060.
Prediction interval
A range, e.g. "1,080 MW, likely between 832 and 1,312 MW". An 80% interval should contain the truth 80% of the time.
Conformal (CQR)
Conformalised Quantile Regression: a method that corrects the width of the ranges using a calibration period so that coverage is really 80%.
Ramp
A sudden big change in output (a cloud bank arriving or leaving).
SHAP
A method that shows how much each input contributed to each prediction.
Köppen–Geiger
A standard world climate map (e.g. BWh = hot desert, BSh = hot semi-arid).
UTC / IST
We store all times in UTC; India time IST = UTC + 5:30, used only for display.

CHAPTER 03Solar power basics

Why solar output changes

  • Sun position: zero at night, peak near noon, lower in winter. This part is perfectly predictable.
  • Clouds: the biggest uncertainty. A passing cloud can cut output by half in minutes.
  • Dust and haze (aerosols): reduce sunlight, especially in Rajasthan and Gujarat.
  • Temperature: panels lose about 0.37% of power for every °C hotter. Hot Indian summers cost real output.
  • Monsoon: June–September clouds and rain drop output by 5–12 percentage points of capacity factor.

How sunlight becomes electricity (the physics chain)

flowchart LR
  A[Weather: GHI, DNI, DHI,
temperature, wind] --> B[Sun position
every hour]
  B --> C[Light on tilted panel
Perez model]
  C --> D[Reflection loss
ASHRAE IAM]
  D --> E[Cell temperature
SAPM / Faiman]
  E --> F[DC power
PVWatts, -0.37%/°C]
  F --> G[Losses ~14%
dust, wiring, mismatch]
  G --> H[Inverter DC→AC
96%, clipped at AC limit]
  H --> I[Power as fraction
of capacity]

In plain words: find where the sun is, work out how much light lands on the tilted panels, remove light reflected away at steep angles, work out how hot the cells get, convert to DC power (less when hot), subtract everyday losses, then pass through the inverter, which cannot exceed the plant's AC rating ("clipping").

Forecasting approaches

  • Physical: weather forecast → physics → power. Good for hours to days, but inherits forecast errors.
  • Statistical / machine learning: learn patterns from history. Strong for the next few hours.
  • Hybrid (ours): feed the ML model both recent output and the weather forecast passed through physics. The ML learns to correct the physics.

Why "solar park" and not "solar power plant"

A park is the shared infrastructure zone; plants are the individual projects inside it built by different companies. MNRE sanctions parks. Using the right word shows you understand the scheme.

CHAPTER 05Research gap and questions

What earlier work was missing:

  • One plant at a time. Most Indian studies forecast a single plant, so we don't know if methods work across deserts, coasts and hills.
  • Real power data is hidden. Parks don't publish hourly output, so most methods can't even be tried.
  • New parks are not tested. Models are rarely tested on a park they never saw, though new parks open every year.
  • No India-wide picture. Few studies measure whether adding parks together (State, region, India) makes forecasting easier.
  • Over-stated accuracy. Many studies feed the model the actual weather instead of a forecast. Nobody has tomorrow's actual weather.
  • No trusted ranges. Most forecasts give one number without a calibrated range.

Why April 2024 to September 2026?

Our scope document first said 2022–2025. But honest archived ECMWF forecasts (with a known issue time) start only in mid-March 2024 for India. An older archive goes back to 2022, but it stitches together the newest forecast for each hour, so it can contain information issued after the forecast time (a hidden cheat). We chose honesty over length: 1 April 2024 – 24 September 2026 (21,768 hours per location).

CHAPTER 07Making the "actual" power

Because no park publishes hourly generation, we simulate it: ERA5 real weather → pvlib physics chain (chapter 3) → hourly power for every park. This is called the target (what the model tries to predict).

Assumptions (same for every park unless stated)

SettingValueWhy
Panel tiltEqual to latitude, facing south (floating: 10°)Standard fixed-tilt design in India
DC/AC ratio1.25Typical Indian utility plant
Temperature coefficient−0.37 %/°CCrystalline silicon panels
Cell temperature modelSAPM (ground); Faiman with stronger cooling (floating)Water cools floating panels
System losses≈14% (PVWatts defaults)Dust, shading, wiring, mismatch, availability
Desert parks (Rajasthan, Gujarat)Soiling 4%, albedo 0.25More dust; brighter sand reflects more
Inverter96% efficient, clipped at AC capacityOutput can never exceed rating

The power is stored as a fraction of capacity (0 to 1). This lets big and small parks share one model; MW = fraction × capacity.

Quality checks (all passed)

  • No missing values; zero output at night; never above capacity.
  • No GHI above physical limits; diffuse never above total.
  • Annual capacity factor 19.8% (Kasaragod, Kerala) to 24.3% (Bhadla, Rajasthan); core-park median 21.6%. Desert parks highest.
  • Monsoon drop: ~6 points in Rajasthan (27% → 21%), 11–12 points in central India and Maharashtra.

How good is the weather forecast itself?

Day-1 ECMWF GHI vs ERA5: correlation 0.90–0.97, error (RMSE) 70–124 W/m². The forecast under-estimates sunlight in Rajasthan (−30 to −37 W/m²) and over-estimates it in the south and centre (+20 to +42; Mizoram +51), probably because of different dust/aerosol handling. A location-dependent bias like this is exactly what machine learning can learn to correct.

Be upfront about this

Our "actual" values are simulated, so results describe how predictable the weather-driven output is, not real plant operations (outages, curtailment). That is why we did the real-plant check in E11.

CHAPTER 08The no-cheating rules

These are the most important design decisions. Examiners like them because they show the results are honest.

Rule 1: Different weather for answers and inputs

The answer (target) is made from ERA5, the real weather. The model's inputs are ECMWF forecasts. If both came from the same weather, the model could simply learn the physics formula and look perfect.

Rule 2: The lead-time rule

A forecast from the previous_dayN series was issued about N×24 hours before its valid time. Forecasts also arrive ~6 hours after they are issued. So for a forecast made at time t for h hours ahead:

usable only if 24 × N − h ≥ 6 hours
  • 1, 3, 6 h ahead → previous_day1 (24 − 6 = 18 ≥ 6 ✓)
  • 24 h ahead → previous_day1 fails (24 − 24 = 0), so we use previous_day2 (48 − 24 = 24 ≥ 6 ✓)

This is conservative: our model never uses a forecast fresher than what an operator would really have.

Rule 3: Recent power only up to the forecast time

For a forecast at time t for t + h, every past-power input is shifted so it ends at t. The only inputs at the future time are things known in advance: sun position, clear-sky power, and the day-old weather forecast.

Rule 4: Time is never shuffled

Train on the past, test on the future. Random shuffling would let the model peek at neighbouring hours from the test period.

Time convention

All timestamps are UTC and mark the end of the hour. Sun position is computed at the middle of the hour (t − 30 min). The website shows IST, so labels end in ":30" (e.g. 12:30 IST = 07:00 UTC).

CHAPTER 10Model inputs (features)

Each row = one park, one future hour. About 1.6 million rows per horizon. Only daylight hours (sun elevation above 5°) are used for training and scoring.

GroupInputsKnown when?
Recent power (13)Power at t, t−1, t−2, t−3, t−6; same hour yesterday; 3/6/24-hour averages; 3/6-hour spread; current clear-sky index and its changeUp to forecast time t
Sun and time (8)Clear-sky power, sun elevation, zenith, azimuth at the target hour; hour and day-of-year as sine/cosineAlways known (astronomy)
Weather forecast (10)ECMWF forecast GHI, DNI, DHI, temperature, humidity, cloud cover, wind, pressure, rain at the target hour + physics power from the forecastIssued 1–2 days earlier
Park metadata (6)Latitude, longitude, log capacity, floating or not, grid region, park IDFixed
Why sine and cosine for hour?

Hour 23 and hour 0 are neighbours, but as plain numbers they look far apart. Putting the hour on a circle (sine, cosine) tells the model that the day wraps around.

Why "physics power from the forecast"?

It gives the model a ready-made first guess. The ML then only has to learn the correction (forecast bias, local effects), which is much easier than learning physics from scratch.

A fifth group, oracle inputs (the ERA5 real weather at the target hour), is used only in experiment E9 to measure how much error is due to the weather forecast. It is never used in normal models.

Machine learning

Ridge regression
A straight-line (linear) model with a penalty that stops weights growing too large. Simple and fast; can't capture curved relationships.
Random Forest
150 decision trees, each trained on a random 30% of rows; the answer is their average. Robust, CPU only.
LightGBM (main)
Gradient boosting: trees are built one after another, each fixing the previous ones' mistakes. Settings: learning rate 0.05, 127 leaves, up to 3,000 trees with early stopping (stops when the calibration error stops improving for 100 rounds). Fast, handles the park ID as a category, supports quantile ranges.
XGBoost (GPU)
Another gradient boosting library, run on the GPU (device="cuda", depth 8). Trains in 2–3 seconds per horizon.
CatBoost (GPU)
Gradient boosting that is good with categories; run on the GPU in E10.

Deep learning (PyTorch, GPU)

LSTM and GRU are recurrent neural networks: they read a sequence step by step and keep a memory. Our network:

  • Reads the last 24 hours of [power, clear-sky power, clear-sky index] with a 2-layer LSTM/GRU of 64 units.
  • Combines that memory with all the tabular inputs (forecast, sun, metadata) and an 8-number learned "park embedding" (a learned fingerprint of each park).
  • Passes through dense layers 128 → 64 → 1 with a sigmoid, so output stays between 0 and 1.
  • Adam optimiser, learning rate 0.001, early stopping on the calibration period. About 20–30 seconds per model on the RTX 4060.

Ensemble

The simple average of LightGBM and GRU. Different model types make slightly different mistakes, so averaging cancels some of them. It gave our best numbers.

Why one global model?

One model trained on all 43 parks sees far more cloudy days, monsoon onsets and forecast errors than any single park. It also works for a park with no history, because it learned general rules.

CHAPTER 12How we tested

PeriodDatesUse
Train1 Apr 2024 – 30 Jun 2025Models learn here
Calibration1 Jul – 31 Dec 2025Early stopping, tuning, calibrating the ranges
Test1 Jan – 24 Sep 2026Scored once; never seen in training

Every period contains a monsoon, so the model is tested on hard weather.

Metrics

nRMSE = √( mean( (actual − forecast)² ) ) ÷ capacity × 100%
Skill = 1 − nRMSE_model ÷ nRMSE_smart persistence

We report nRMSE as a percent of capacity, over daylight hours only (night is trivially zero and would make everything look better). MAPE (percent error) is not used near sunrise and sunset because dividing by almost zero makes it meaningless.

Example

Pavagada (2,000 MW). nRMSE 4.5% → typical error ≈ 90 MW. Smart persistence 5.3% → ≈ 106 MW. Skill = 1 − 4.5/5.3 ≈ 15%.

What it means:

  • Every learned model beats every baseline at every horizon.
  • The advantage grows with horizon up to 6 h: recent power says less about the far future, while the weather forecast stays useful.
  • Why is plain persistence better at 24 h (9.68) than at 6 h? Because "same as 24 hours ago" = same hour yesterday, so the sun is in the same place. At 6 h, "same as now" compares morning with afternoon.

How to say it

"All machine-learning models beat the baselines, by 15% at one hour up to 44% at six hours. The surprising part is that Random Forest, boosting and LSTM are almost equal, so better inputs, not fancier models, are the way forward."

E9 · How much error is the weather forecast's fault?

Give the model the real weather (ERA5) instead of the forecast: error drops to about 0.35–0.37%, more than ten times smaller. So almost all remaining error comes from the weather forecast, not the model. (This "oracle" model is only a diagnostic; it can't be used in practice.)

Why: forecast errors at far-apart parks are independent. Error correlation: 0.62 within 100 km, 0.17 at 100–300 km, 0.02 at 300–600 km, ~0 beyond. How to say it

"A cloud over Rajasthan has nothing to do with a cloud over Karnataka, so errors cancel. Forecasting India's total is three times easier than forecasting one park."

A ramp = the clear-sky index jumps by more than 0.3 in one hour while there is meaningful sunlight, or a change above the park's 95th percentile. The model makes ramp errors smaller, but cannot predict that a ramp will happen: probability of detection 0.04, false-alarm ratio 0.89, critical success index 0.03. Reason: single clouds are much smaller than the 25 km forecast grid. Satellite nowcasting is needed for this.

Partly cloudy days are harder than fully overcast ones because broken clouds change quickly; overcast is dull but steady.

E7 · Can we forecast a park we've never seen?

Leave-one-location-out: hide one weather location (and all parks there) from training, train on the rest, then forecast the hidden parks. Extra error: +0.37 points on average at 1 h (worst +4.2), +0.63 at 6 h (worst +11.3). Worst cases are parks with unusual climates. So new parks can get good forecasts from day one, as long as their climate is represented.

Co-located parks (e.g. Bhadla II–IV) are hidden together, otherwise the model would have seen the same weather.

How CQR works, simply: train models for the 10th and 90th percentiles. On the calibration period, see how often the truth falls outside, and by how much. Widen (or narrow) every interval by the amount that makes exactly 80% of calibration cases fall inside. The raw ranges were over-confident (70%); CQR fixes it. Monthly coverage stays 76–85%, a little low in the monsoon. Quantiles can't simply be added across parks, so the all-parks total gets its own split-conformal interval.

E11 · Does it work on real plants?

Public Kaggle data: two Indian plants, 34 days (15 May – 17 Jun 2020), measured output and on-site sensors. Locations aren't published, so we couldn't match ECMWF forecasts; capacity isn't published, so we used the 99.5th percentile of output as effective capacity.

Part A, physics check: pvlib on the plant's own sunlight sensor vs measured power: Plant 1 correlation 0.992, error 3.4% after calibration. Plant 2: 0.821, 16.0%, because its data has outages and shutdowns no weather model can explain.

CHAPTER 17Limitations

  • Simulated targets. Most "actual" power is calculated from weather, so it has no outages, curtailment or maintenance.
  • Assumed design. Tilt, DC/AC ratio, trackers and losses are not published per park; we used typical values.
  • Capacity over time. Commissioning after Oct 2023 is not published per park, so core parks use 2023 commissioned capacity (a lower bound).
  • Approximate coordinates; some parks are spread over several sites.
  • Coarse weather grid (25 km) can't see individual clouds, so ramps are missed.
  • Hourly, not 15-minute. Indian scheduling uses 15-minute blocks.
  • ERA5 is itself a model, not a ground measurement.
  • Real-data test is small: two plants, 34 days, without matching weather forecasts.
  • Köppen map artefact: seven central-Indian parks fall in a "Mediterranean" class.
  • Not live yet: website shows saved test-period forecasts.

CHAPTER 18Future scope

  • Satellite nowcasting (INSAT-3D/3DR, Meteosat) for 0–2 hours: the best fix for ramps and the main remaining error source (E6, E9).
  • Run live: download each new ECMWF forecast automatically and update the website every hour.
  • 15-minute forecasts to match Indian scheduling and deviation settlement (CERC rules).
  • Real data from park operators or load-despatch centres (SLDC/RLDC) to replace simulated targets.
  • Adaptive conformal intervals that adjust in the monsoon (coverage dips to ~76%).
  • Bigger models: Temporal Fusion Transformer, N-HiTS, graph neural networks across parks; blend several weather models.
  • Hierarchical reconciliation so park, State and India forecasts add up consistently with their ranges.
  • Extend to hybrid wind–solar parks and all solar plants in a State.

CHAPTER 19Viva questions and answers

Why did you change from one plant to all MNRE parks?

Single-plant results don't generalise across India's climates, grid operators care about State and regional totals, and data scarcity is the real Indian problem. Many parks let us study generalisation and aggregation, which one plant can't.

The brief said 42 parks. Why do you have 50, and why 43?

42 parks (23,499 MW) is correct for 31 March 2019 (SECI annual report). The latest official park-wise list (Lok Sabha Q.3633, Aug 2023) has 50 parks, 37,990 MW. We model the 43 that have a verified location and are not dropped.

If the power is simulated, aren't you just predicting your own simulator?

No, for two reasons. First, the target comes from the real weather (ERA5) while the model only sees day-old forecasts; it must predict the weather's effect, not copy a formula. Second, on two real plants the model, without retraining, beat smart persistence. We still label results as forecastability of weather-driven output.

Why is smart persistence the main baseline?

It's the standard reference in solar forecasting. It already knows the sun's movement and only assumes cloudiness stays the same, so beating it proves the model adds real weather knowledge.

Why did deep learning not beat LightGBM?

Because the limit is the information in the inputs, mainly the weather forecast (E9 shows perfect weather would cut error tenfold). Our inputs are already well-designed tabular features where boosted trees are very strong. LSTM and GRU matched LightGBM, and averaging them gave a small gain.

Why use the GPU?

XGBoost, CatBoost and the neural networks train much faster on the RTX 4060 (XGBoost 2–3 s per horizon, each LSTM/GRU 20–30 s), which let us run many experiments. LightGBM and Random Forest ran on the CPU.

What is the lead-time rule?

A forecast issued N days before its valid time can be used for horizon h only if 24N − h ≥ 6 hours, allowing ~6 hours for the forecast to become available. 1–6 h use day-1 forecasts; 24 h uses day-2 forecasts.

Why did you not use 2022–2023 data?

Honest archived ECMWF forecasts with a known issue time start in March 2024 for India. An older archive exists but can contain information issued after the forecast time. We chose honesty over a longer period.

Why does a global model beat per-park models?

It learns from 43 times more weather situations (cloud events, monsoon onsets, forecast errors). Rare events at one park are common across all parks. It also handles new parks.

Why is persistence better at 24 h than at 6 h?

At 24 h, "same as now" means same hour yesterday, so the sun is in the same place. At 6 h it compares, say, 9 AM with 3 PM, which is completely different sun.

Why does aggregation reduce error?

Errors at distant parks are nearly uncorrelated (0.02 beyond 300 km), so they partly cancel when summed. Total variance = sum of variances + 2 × sum of covariances; small covariance means a small total.

What is conformal prediction and why do you need it?

The raw quantile model's 80% ranges only contained the truth 70% of the time. Conformal calibration uses a separate calibration period to measure that miss rate and widens the ranges until coverage is 80%. Result: 80–82%.

Why can't your model predict ramps?

Ramps come from individual clouds, much smaller than the 25 km weather grid and faster than hourly data. The model reduces their size (21.8 vs 27.1) but can't foresee them. Satellite images are needed.

What is nRMSE and why percent of capacity?

Root-mean-square error divided by installed capacity. It lets us compare parks of different sizes fairly, and is the standard in solar forecasting and in Indian deviation rules (which are capacity-based).

Why only daylight hours?

At night output is zero and trivially predictable; including it would make errors look artificially small.

How did you ensure no data leakage?

Different weather for targets and inputs; the lead-time rule; past power shifted to the forecast time; chronological split, test period scored once; co-located parks held out together in E7; ERA5 inputs used only in the diagnostic oracle experiment.

What did SHAP show?

Weather forecast inputs contribute 54% (1 h) rising to 71% (6 h); recent power 36% falling to 19%; sun/time ~8%; metadata 1–3%.

What would you do with more time?

Satellite nowcasting, live hourly operation, 15-minute forecasts, real operator data, adaptive intervals for the monsoon.
